Ask an MEP project manager what delayed their last project and you will rarely hear "installation was slow". You are much more likely to hear about a chiller that arrived late, switchgear stuck in approval, or a fire pump that had to be resubmitted twice.

On MEP work, the programme on the wall shows installation. The real critical path often runs through something the programme barely shows: **getting materials approved, ordered and delivered.**

## The approval chain nobody schedules

For major equipment, a typical sequence looks like this:

1. The contractor prepares a material submittal with datasheets, compliance statements and samples.
2. The consultant reviews it and approves, approves with comments, or rejects it.
3. If rejected, the contractor revises and resubmits, and the review starts again.
4. Once approved, the PO is placed with the supplier.
5. The supplier manufactures and ships, which for large equipment can take months.
6. The equipment arrives, is inspected, stored and finally installed.

Each step has its own duration and its own owner. But on most programmes, the whole chain is one line or missing entirely, and installation starts from a date that assumes everything went smoothly.

## Work backwards from the installation date

The simplest improvement is to schedule approvals in reverse. For every long-lead item, start from the date it needs to be installed and work back:

- **Installation date**, from the programme.
- **Minus delivery and storage buffer.** When it needs to be on site.
- **Minus manufacturing and shipping lead time.** When the PO must be placed.
- **Minus approval time**, including at least one resubmission. When the submittal must go in.

For many items, the answer is uncomfortable: the submittal should have gone in last month. It is much better to find that out in the first weeks of the project than when the ceiling contractor is waiting for the AHUs.

## Track every submittal as a live item

A submittal log in a spreadsheet shows status. What you need is a list that shows **risk**. For each submittal, track:

- **The item and the BOQ line it belongs to**, so commercial and site teams are talking about the same thing.
- **Current status and who it is waiting on**: you, the consultant or the supplier.
- **Days in review**, compared with the time allowed.
- **The date it must be approved** to protect the installation date.
- **Revision number**, because a third resubmission is a warning sign on its own.

Sorted by required approval date, this becomes the most important list in the weekly meeting.

## Reduce rejections before you submit

Every rejection costs weeks. Most are avoidable:

- **Check the specification line by line** before submitting. Many rejections come from a missing compliance statement or a small mismatch with the specified rating.
- **Submit alternatives early.** If you plan to propose an equivalent product, raise it before the deadline, not with it.
- **Keep approved submittals from past projects.** If a consultant approved a product last year, the same package is a strong starting point.
- **Talk before you submit.** A short call with the consultant's reviewer on a complex item can save a full review cycle.

## Connect approvals to procurement and site

The approval date is only useful if it drives what happens next. Once a submittal is approved, procurement should know the same day that the PO can be placed. When the PO is placed, the expected delivery date should be visible to the site team planning the installation.

When these steps live in different people's inboxes, days are lost at every hand-off. When they are connected, a delay at any step is visible to everyone who depends on it.

## A weekly habit that helps

Once a week, the PM, procurement lead and MEP coordinator spend fifteen minutes on one question: **which approvals are now late enough to threaten an installation date?** For each one, agree a single action and an owner.

It is not complicated. But on most of the projects we see, it is the difference between finding out about a late chiller in month two and finding out in month eight.
