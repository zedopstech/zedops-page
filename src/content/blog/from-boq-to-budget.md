On most MEP projects, the same scope of work gets described three times.

First, the estimator prices it in the tender BOQ. Then, after award, someone rebuilds it as a budget in a spreadsheet, usually with a different structure. Then the site team describes it a third time in their programme, material requests and progress reports, using whatever names make sense on site.

Each version is reasonable on its own. Together, they make it almost impossible to answer the simplest commercial question on a live job: **are we making or losing money on this piece of work?**

## Where the line breaks

The break usually happens at award. The tender team moves on to the next bid. The project team inherits a priced BOQ in a format built for the client, not for running the job.

So the QS rebuilds it. Items get merged, split or renamed. Preliminaries get spread differently. By the time the budget is ready, it no longer maps cleanly to the tender, and nobody can say with confidence where the original allowance for, say, the chilled water pipework went.

From then on, every comparison between what was priced and what is being spent needs manual work, and manual work gets done monthly at best.

## What it costs you

The cost isn't just the QS's time, although that is real. The bigger costs are:

- **Late warnings.** If you only compare actual cost to the tender once a month, overruns are four weeks old by the time you see them.
- **Weak variation claims.** When extra work is instructed, you need to show what was in the original scope and what wasn't. That is hard when the scope has been re-described twice.
- **Lost learning.** When the job finishes, you can't feed real costs back into the rates for the next tender, because the lines don't match.

## Keep one line from tender to final account

The fix is simple to describe: **the BOQ you win the job with becomes the structure you run the job on.**

That means:

1. **The priced BOQ becomes the budget baseline.** No re-keying. Each BOQ item carries its quantity, rate and total into the project as the original budget.
2. **Every commitment links back to a BOQ item.** When site raises a material request and procurement issues a PO, it is recorded against the item it belongs to.
3. **Every actual cost links back too.** Deliveries, subcontractor valuations and labour all land against the same lines.
4. **Variations are new lines, not edits.** Instructed changes are added alongside the original scope, so you can always see what was priced and what was added.

With that structure, "budget versus committed versus actual" is not a monthly report. It is a live view you can open any day.

## It starts with how you estimate

This only works if the estimate is built with the job in mind, not just the client's format. A few habits help:

- **Break items down the way the site builds.** By system and by area or floor, not one line for "all ductwork".
- **Keep your own rate library.** Rates that come from your own completed projects are more reliable than rates copied from the last tender.
- **Record your assumptions.** Exclusions, provisional sums and quantities you measured versus quantities you were given. They matter when variations arrive.

## What it looks like on site

When the BOQ runs through to the site, the site team gets something useful too. A site engineer raising a material request can see how much of that item has already been requested. A storekeeper receiving a delivery knows which part of the job it is for. The PM can see which areas are consuming material faster than planned, before it becomes a cost problem.

That is the real benefit of one line of cost. It lets commercial problems show up in the field while there is still time to do something about them.
