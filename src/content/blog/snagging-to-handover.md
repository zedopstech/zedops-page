Anyone who has closed out an MEP project knows the last month. Hundreds of open snags. Testing and commissioning records spread across emails and folders. O&M manuals being assembled from supplier PDFs the week before handover. As-built drawings that are only nearly as-built.

None of this is surprising, and almost none of it is unavoidable. The work to hand over a project happens throughout the job. The problem is that the evidence is collected at the end.

## Why handover goes wrong

Handover packages fail for predictable reasons:

- **Snags are logged in too many places.** The consultant's walk-through list, the site engineer's notebook, a subcontractor's WhatsApp group. Nobody has the full picture.
- **Testing records are separate from the work.** A pressure test certificate exists somewhere, but nobody can quickly link it to the system and area it covers.
- **Documents arrive late.** Warranties, datasheets and O&M information are requested from suppliers at the end, when they have moved on to other jobs.
- **Nobody owns the package.** Everyone contributes, but no one is responsible for it being complete.

## Treat snags as tasks, not a list

A snag list is only useful if each item can be closed. That means every snag needs:

1. **A location.** Floor, room or grid, ideally pinned on the drawing.
2. **A photo** of the problem.
3. **A trade and an owner**, so it goes to the right subcontractor.
4. **A due date.**
5. **A closing photo** showing it fixed, before it is marked complete.

Logged this way from the first inspection, snags stop being one enormous list at the end and become a steady flow of small tasks that close as the job progresses. The final walk-through then finds the handful of issues that were missed, not the hundreds that were never tracked.

## Capture testing as you go

Testing and commissioning happen in stages: pressure tests before insulation, insulation resistance tests before energising, flushing, balancing and final commissioning.

Each test should be recorded against the system and area it covers, on the day it happens, with the signed record attached. Then when the client asks for proof that the Level 3 chilled water was pressure tested, the answer takes seconds, not a search through the archive.

This matters beyond handover. Authorities, consultants and facility managers all ask for these records, sometimes years later.

## Collect documents when you buy, not when you finish

The best time to get a datasheet, warranty certificate or O&M information from a supplier is when you place the order, while they want your business.

Make it part of procurement. When a PO is issued for major equipment, the required handover documents are listed with it. When the equipment is delivered, the documents are checked along with the goods. By the end of the job, most of the O&M package already exists.

## Keep as-builts current

As-built drawings are hard to reconstruct at the end because the knowledge is spread across the people who made the changes. Ask site teams to mark deviations on the drawings when they happen. A quick markup with a photo is enough. The drafting team can turn those into formal as-builts gradually, not all in the final month.

## Give the handover package an owner

Finally, name one person responsible for handover from the start of the project. Not to do all the work, but to track whether it is being done: snags closing, tests being recorded, documents arriving.

A simple monthly check on those three numbers tells you, months in advance, whether handover will be smooth or painful.
